Getting paid faster with reminder schedules

A practical reminder schedule for broker-billed premiums and fees: when to send, what to say, when to escalate and how to keep trust accounting clean along the way.

  • Operations
  • Payments
  • Accounting
  • Client service

Late payments are a process problem

With agency bill, often called broker bill in Canada, the brokerage invoices the client, collects the premium, holds it in trust and pays the insurer by the due date on the insurer's statement. When clients pay late, the brokerage carries the gap: chasing payments, explaining balances and, in the worst case, managing a cancellation for non-payment. Direct-billed policies shift collection to the insurer, but clients still call their broker when a payment fails.

Most late payments aren't refusals. They're forgotten invoices, expired cards and amounts nobody explained. A consistent reminder schedule, paired with easy ways to pay, solves much of the problem without awkward phone calls.

An example schedule for broker-billed invoices

Adapt this to your payment terms and your insurers' remittance dates. The aim is a rhythm clients recognize and staff don't have to remember.

  1. On issue: send the invoice with the amount, due date, what it covers and a link to pay online.
  2. Seven days before the due date: a friendly reminder with the same link. Many payments arrive here.
  3. On the due date: a short note that payment is due today.
  4. Seven days after the due date: a firmer reminder that the payment is overdue, with a direct contact for questions.
  5. Fourteen days after the due date: a personal call or email from the client's CSR or producer. Something may have changed.
  6. Well before any insurer deadline: escalate to the producer and accounting, and follow your brokerage's procedure for notices and possible cancellation.

What to say, and how

Reminders work best when they're short, specific and easy to act on. Every message should include the client's name, the policy and line of business, the amount, the due date and a way to pay. Skip internal jargon. "Additional premium on endorsement" means little to most clients, while "the extra premium for adding your new vehicle" is clear.

Keep the tone helpful, especially early in the schedule. A client who feels ambushed by a payment demand calls to complain. A client who gets a clear reminder with a payment link usually just pays.

Make paying the easy part

Every reminder should end with an easy way to pay. Online card payments and pre-authorized debit (PAD) remove most of the friction. For clients on monthly payment plans, PAD reduces missed payments, though a failed debit still needs prompt follow-up. For larger commercial premiums, premium financing can help a client spread the cost where it suits the account.

Whatever methods you offer, make sure each payment lands against the right invoice and policy automatically. Unapplied cash, where money arrives but nobody knows what it's for, slows down reconciliation and creates awkward client conversations.

Choose channels with consent in mind

Email suits invoices and statements. Text messages get read quickly and work well for short reminders with a payment link. Under CASL, factual messages about an existing account may be exempt from the consent requirement, but they still need to identify your brokerage and include an unsubscribe mechanism. Respect each client's contact preferences, and keep promotions out of payment reminders, since mixing them in can make the whole message commercial.

Escalate before it becomes a cancellation

Non-payment on a broker-billed policy can eventually lead to cancellation, and insurers and provincial insurance law set requirements for how and when notice is given. Agree an internal escalation path well before that point: who calls the client, when the producer is told and when accounting raises it with the insurer. Record every contact on the client file. If a policy is cancelled, the record shows the client was informed and given chances to pay.

Keep trust accounting clean

Faster payment helps trust accounting directly. Premiums collected from clients are generally held in trust until they're paid to the insurer, and provincial rules govern how trust accounts are kept and reconciled. When reminders, payments and receipts all connect to the same invoice, reconciliation is quicker and the trust position is easier to see at any point in the month.

Measure the results

  • Average days from invoice to payment
  • Share of invoices paid by the due date
  • Share of payments made online or by PAD
  • Number of policies reaching a final notice each month

Review these monthly. If most payments arrive after the second reminder, move it earlier. If clients on a particular line or with a particular producer tend to pay late, look at how those invoices are explained at the point of sale.

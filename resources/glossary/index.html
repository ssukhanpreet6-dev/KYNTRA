A

Abeyance
A dated follow-up reminder attached to a client, policy or claim, so that something gets done on time: chasing a signed form, reviewing a renewal, confirming an endorsement with the insurer. Abeyances keep work from depending on anyone's memory, and they need to be reassigned when someone is away so nothing slips.Related:Customer service representative (CSR)RenewalEndorsement
Account rounding
Adding the other policies a client needs to an existing relationship, such as auto for a home client, a personal umbrella, or coverage for a home-based business. Rounding an account closes coverage gaps, and brokers often find that clients with several policies are more likely to stay.Related:Personal linesUmbrella liabilityRenewal retention
Actual cash value (ACV)
A basis of claim settlement that pays the cost to replace damaged property minus depreciation for age and wear. ACV settlements are usually lower than replacement cost settlements for the same loss. The policy sets out which basis applies to which property.Related:Replacement costDeductibleClaim
Additional insured
A person or organization added to someone else's policy so they have protection under it, usually for liability arising from the named insured's work or premises. Common in commercial lines, where contracts and leases often require a contractor or tenant to add the other party as an additional insured.Related:Named insuredCertificate of insuranceCommercial general liability (CGL)
Adjuster
The person who investigates a claim, assesses the damage and the coverage, and recommends or settles payment. Adjusters may be employed by the insurer or work for an independent adjusting firm the insurer hires.Related:ClaimFirst notice of loss (FNOL)
Agency bill (broker bill)
A billing arrangement where the brokerage invoices the client, collects the premium and pays the insurer, usually net of the brokerage's commission and according to the insurer's statement. In Canada it's often called broker bill. Premiums collected are generally held in trust until they're paid to the insurer.Related:Direct bill (company bill)Trust accountingInsurer statement (account current)Commission

B

Binder
Temporary written evidence that coverage is in force, issued by an insurer, or by a broker with binding authority, until the formal policy is issued. A binder sets out the key terms, such as the insured, the property or vehicle, coverages, limits and effective date.Related:Binding authorityDeclarations page (dec page)Effective date
Binding authority
The authority an insurer gives a brokerage to put coverage in force on its behalf, within set limits for lines of business, types of risk and amounts. It's defined in the agreement between the insurer and the brokerage. Anything outside it must be referred to the insurer's underwriter.Related:BinderUnderwriterInsurer
Book of business
All the clients and policies a brokerage, or an individual producer, looks after. A book is usually measured by policy count, premium and commission, and broken down by line of business, insurer and producer.Related:ProducerRenewal retentionCommission
Broker of record (BOR)
The brokerage an insurer recognizes as servicing a particular policy. A client can change broker of record by signing a broker of record letter that authorizes a new brokerage to act for them. The insurer then moves servicing, and usually commission, to the new brokerage from an effective date. Insurers' rules for accepting a change vary, especially mid-term.Related:Named insuredCommissionInsurer

C

Cancellation
Ending a policy before its expiry date, at the request of the insured or the insurer. Any unearned premium is refunded on a pro rata or short-rate basis, depending on who cancels and the policy terms. An insurer must give the notice required by the policy and provincial insurance law before cancelling.Related:Pro rata cancellationShort-rate cancellationReinstatement
CASL
Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation, which sets rules for sending commercial electronic messages such as marketing emails and texts. In general, a commercial message needs the recipient's consent, identification of the sender and a working unsubscribe mechanism. This is general information, not legal advice.Related:Express consent (CASL)Implied consent (CASL)PIPEDA
Certificate of insurance
A document summarizing coverage in force under a policy, issued to a third party who needs proof, such as a landlord, general contractor or event venue. It confirms coverage at the time it's issued but doesn't change the policy or give the holder rights unless the policy itself is endorsed.Related:Additional insuredCommercial general liability (CGL)Endorsement
Claim
A request for payment or services under a policy after a loss, made by the insured or by a third party. Brokers typically help clients report claims, follow progress with the adjuster and keep claim notes on the client file.Related:First notice of loss (FNOL)AdjusterDeductibleLoss history
Client portal
A secure website or app where a brokerage's clients can view their policies, download documents such as liability cards, request changes and make payments without calling the office.Related:Pink card (liability insurance card)Pre-authorized debit (PAD)Customer service representative (CSR)
Coinsurance clause
A condition in many commercial property policies requiring the insured to carry a limit of at least a set percentage of the property's value, often 80 or 90 percent. If the limit is lower, partial losses are paid in proportion to the shortfall. It's one reason accurate valuations matter at every renewal.Related:Insurance to value (ITV)Schedule of valuesReplacement cost
Commercial general liability (CGL)
A policy covering a business's legal liability for bodily injury and property damage to others arising from its premises, operations, products and completed work, along with defence costs. Contracts and leases often require it.Related:Additional insuredCertificate of insuranceUmbrella liabilityCommercial lines
Commercial lines
Insurance for businesses and organizations, such as commercial property, general liability, commercial auto, equipment and professional liability. Commercial accounts are usually more complex than personal lines, with submissions, underwriting questions and longer renewal cycles.Related:Personal linesSubmissionCommercial general liability (CGL)
Commission
The payment an insurer makes to a brokerage for placing and servicing a policy, usually a percentage of the premium. Rates vary by insurer and line of business. With agency bill, the brokerage usually keeps its commission from the premium it collects. With direct bill, the insurer pays commission on a statement.Related:Contingent commissionAgency bill (broker bill)Direct bill (company bill)
Comparative rater
Software that takes a risk's details once and returns premiums from several insurers side by side, so a broker can compare options quickly. Results are subject to each insurer's underwriting before coverage is bound.Related:MarketCSIONew business
Contingent commission
Additional compensation an insurer may pay a brokerage based on the results of the business placed with it, such as volume, growth, retention or loss ratio, usually calculated once a year. Also called profit-sharing or bonus commission. Disclosure expectations for these arrangements are set by regulators and vary by province.Related:CommissionLoss ratioBook of business
CSIO
The Centre for Study of Insurance Operations, a not-for-profit organization that develops data and technology standards for the Canadian property and casualty insurance industry. CSIO standards let insurers, brokers and software providers exchange policy data, quotes, transactions and documents without rekeying.Related:Policy downloadeDocsComparative rater
Customer service representative (CSR)
The brokerage team member who services clients day to day: processing endorsements, answering coverage and billing questions, issuing documents and handling renewals. CSRs who advise on or arrange insurance generally need a provincial licence.Related:ProducerAbeyanceLicensed insurance broker

D

Declarations page (dec page)
The summary page of a policy, listing the named insured, policy number, policy period, insured property or vehicles, coverages, limits, deductibles and premium. It's usually the first document a client or broker checks when a question comes up.Related:Named insuredDeductibleLimitBinder
Deductible
The part of a covered loss the insured pays before the insurer pays the rest. A higher deductible usually means a lower premium. A policy can carry different deductibles for different perils, such as a separate deductible for water damage or earthquake.Related:LimitClaimPremium
Direct bill (company bill)
A billing arrangement where the insurer invoices and collects from the client directly, often by monthly pre-authorized debit, and pays the brokerage its commission separately. The broker still services the policy and is usually the client's first call when a payment problem comes up.Related:Agency bill (broker bill)Pre-authorized debit (PAD)Commission

E

eDocs
CSIO's standard for delivering policy documents, such as declarations pages, renewal documents and liability cards, electronically from insurers into a broker management system, filed to the right client and policy. Availability varies by insurer.Related:CSIOPolicy downloadDeclarations page (dec page)
Effective date
The date, and often the time, that coverage begins, whether for a new policy, a renewal or an endorsement. Together with the expiry date, it defines the policy period.Related:RenewalBinderEndorsement
Endorsement
A change to a policy during its term, such as adding a vehicle, changing an address or increasing a limit, or a form that adds, removes or modifies coverage. An endorsement can change the premium, creating an additional or returned premium.Related:Declarations page (dec page)Policy downloadAbeyance
Errors and omissions (E&O) insurance
Professional liability insurance that protects a brokerage and its brokers if a client alleges financial loss caused by a mistake, oversight or failure to provide a service, such as not offering a coverage or not processing a change. Provincial regulators generally require licensed brokers to carry it. Good documentation is central to managing E&O risk.Related:Signed declinationLicensed insurance brokerAbeyance
Exclusion
A provision that removes coverage for specific causes of loss, property or situations, such as wear and tear or intentional acts. Exclusions are a common source of client questions, so brokers refer to the policy wording when explaining them.Related:EndorsementDeclarations page (dec page)
Express consent (CASL)
Clear, active permission to receive commercial electronic messages, given in response to a request that explains the purpose, identifies who is asking and says consent can be withdrawn at any time. It doesn't expire until it's withdrawn. General information, not legal advice.Related:CASLImplied consent (CASL)

F

First notice of loss (FNOL)
The first report of a loss to the insurer, which starts the claim. It records what happened, when and where, who was involved and what was damaged. Policies require losses to be reported promptly, and complete information helps the claim move faster.Related:ClaimAdjuster

I

Implied consent (CASL)
Consent the law infers in specific situations, most commonly an existing business relationship: generally a purchase or contract within the past two years, or an inquiry within the past six months. Because implied consent is time-limited, brokerages track when it expires. General information, not legal advice.Related:CASLExpress consent (CASL)
Insurance to value (ITV)
How closely a policy's limit matches the full cost to rebuild or replace the insured property. A home or building insured well below its replacement cost leaves the client exposed on a large loss, and can trigger a coinsurance penalty on commercial property.Related:Replacement costCoinsurance clauseSchedule of values
Insurer
The company that issues a policy and pays covered claims. Brokers in Canada typically represent several insurers, which lets them compare options for each client. In everyday brokerage language, insurers are also called companies or markets.Related:UnderwriterMarketBinding authority
Insurer statement (account current)
The periodic statement an insurer sends a brokerage listing broker-billed transactions, such as new business, endorsements and cancellations, with the premium and commission on each and the net amount due. Brokerages reconcile it against their own records before paying.Related:Agency bill (broker bill)Trust accountingReconciliation

L

Licensed insurance broker
A person licensed by a provincial regulator to advise on and arrange insurance. Regulators include the Insurance Council of British Columbia, the Alberta Insurance Council and the Registered Insurance Brokers of Ontario (RIBO). Licence types, levels and continuing education requirements vary by province.Related:Errors and omissions (E&O) insurancePrincipalCustomer service representative (CSR)
Limit
The most an insurer will pay under a policy or a particular coverage, per occurrence, per claim or in total over the policy period (an aggregate limit), as set out in the policy.Related:DeductibleDeclarations page (dec page)Umbrella liability
Loss history
A record of a client's past claims, with dates, causes and amounts paid or reserved. Insurers use it to evaluate and price a risk, and for commercial accounts it's a standard part of every submission.Related:ClaimSubmissionLoss ratio
Loss ratio
Claims costs as a share of premium, for a policy, a client, a line of business or a whole book. A brokerage's loss ratio with an insurer can affect that insurer's appetite and, where a contingent commission agreement exists, what it pays.Related:Loss historyContingent commissionBook of business

M

Market
An insurer, or a program within an insurer, available to place a particular type of risk. A market's appetite describes the risks it actively wants to write. Approaching other markets for an existing client is called remarketing.Related:InsurerRemarketingSubmission
Mortgagee
A lender with a financial interest in an insured property, named on the policy so that claim payments protect its interest. The mortgage clause generally requires the insurer to notify the mortgagee before cancelling. Brokers often send proof of insurance to the lender when a property is bought or refinanced.Related:Named insuredCancellationCertificate of insurance

N

Named insured
The person or organization named on the declarations page as the policyholder. The named insured has the main rights and duties under the policy, such as requesting changes, receiving notices and cancelling. Others, such as household members or additional insureds, may be covered without being the named insured.Related:Additional insuredDeclarations page (dec page)Mortgagee
New business
A policy written for a client for the first time, as opposed to a renewal. Brokerages track new business separately to measure growth and producer performance.Related:ProducerRenewalBook of business
Non-renewal
A decision by the insurer, or the client, not to continue a policy past its expiry date. Insurers typically give advance notice of non-renewal, and the requirements depend on the province and line of business. The broker usually needs to find another market before the policy ends.Related:RenewalMarketCancellation

P

Personal lines
Insurance for individuals and households, such as home, condo, tenant, auto, recreational vehicles and personal umbrella. Personal lines are high volume with fairly standard coverage, so efficient servicing and renewal processes matter.Related:Commercial linesRenewalAccount rounding
Pink card (liability insurance card)
Proof of auto insurance issued by the insurer, traditionally printed on pink paper, formally the Canada Inter-Province Motor Vehicle Liability Insurance Card. In provinces where auto insurance is sold by private insurers, drivers must be able to show proof of insurance when asked. Rules on format, including electronic proof, vary by province.Related:Client portaleDocsDeclarations page (dec page)
PIPEDA
The Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act, Canada's federal private-sector privacy law. It governs how organizations collect, use and disclose personal information in commercial activities, except where a substantially similar provincial law applies, such as BC PIPA, Alberta PIPA or Quebec's private-sector law as amended by Law 25.Related:Privacy officerCASL
Policy download
Policy data an insurer sends electronically to a brokerage's management system, such as new business, renewals, endorsements and cancellations, so staff don't have to rekey the insurer's changes. Downloads are usually set up insurer by insurer, and availability varies.Related:CSIOeDocsEndorsement
Pre-authorized debit (PAD)
A payment method where a client authorizes a business to withdraw payments directly from their bank account, on a schedule or as agreed. In Canada, PADs follow Payments Canada rules, including the client's authorization. Common for monthly premium plans.Related:Direct bill (company bill)Premium financePremium
Premium
The amount a client pays for an insurance policy for the policy period. It can change mid-term through endorsements, be partly refunded on cancellation and be paid in full or in instalments.Related:DeductibleEndorsementPremium finance
Premium finance
A loan that lets a client pay a large premium, often a commercial one, in instalments. The finance company pays the premium up front and the client repays the lender, with interest or fees. If the client stops paying, the lender can typically request cancellation of the policy.Related:PremiumPre-authorized debit (PAD)Cancellation
Principal
An owner or senior leader of a brokerage, typically overseeing finances, insurer relationships, staffing and regulatory obligations. Many provincial regulators require a brokerage to name a licensed individual responsible for its conduct; the title for that role varies by province.Related:Licensed insurance brokerProducerBook of business
Privacy officer
The person an organization designates as accountable for its privacy practices, including responding to access requests and complaints. Canadian privacy laws expect organizations to designate someone in this role. General information, not legal advice.Related:PIPEDACASL
Pro rata cancellation
A cancellation where unearned premium is refunded in exact proportion to the unused part of the policy term. Cancelling a one-year policy halfway through, for example, returns half the annual premium. Cancellations made by the insurer are usually calculated pro rata.Related:CancellationShort-rate cancellationPremium
Producer
A licensed broker whose main role is winning new business and developing client relationships, often with responsibility for a book of business. Producers usually work alongside CSRs or account managers who handle day-to-day servicing.Related:Customer service representative (CSR)Book of businessNew business

R

Reconciliation
Matching two sets of records so they agree, such as the brokerage's books against bank and trust account statements, or its receivables against an insurer statement. Regular reconciliation catches errors early and supports trust accounting obligations.Related:Trust accountingInsurer statement (account current)
Reinstatement
Restoring a cancelled or lapsed policy to force, usually once the reason for cancellation, such as non-payment, has been resolved. Whether a policy can be reinstated, and whether there's a gap in coverage, depends on the insurer.Related:CancellationEndorsement
Remarketing
Approaching other insurers for terms on an existing client's coverage, usually at renewal because of a large premium increase, a coverage change or a client request. Good practice is to record the reason, the markets approached, the results and the client's decision.Related:MarketRenewalRenewal retention
Renewal
Continuing a policy for a new term when the current one expires, often with updated premium and terms from the insurer. For brokers, the renewal is a key moment to review coverage with the client, check for changes and decide whether to remarket.Related:Renewal retentionRemarketingNon-renewal
Renewal retention
The share of business due for renewal that a brokerage keeps, usually measured two ways: policy count retention (policies renewed versus policies due) and premium retention (renewed premium versus premium due). Premium retention rising while policy retention falls often means rate increases are masking lost clients.Related:RenewalRemarketingBook of business
Replacement cost
The cost to repair or replace damaged property with materials of like kind and quality, without deducting depreciation. For a home, it's the cost to rebuild, which differs from market value because it excludes land and reflects construction costs. Many policies pay replacement cost only once the property is actually repaired or replaced.Related:Actual cash value (ACV)Insurance to value (ITV)Coinsurance clause

S

Schedule of values
A list of the locations, buildings, contents, equipment and other property covered under a commercial policy, with a value for each. Underwriters use it to price the risk and set limits, and it should be updated at every renewal.Related:Coinsurance clauseInsurance to value (ITV)Submission
Short-rate cancellation
A cancellation where the refund of unearned premium is reduced by a penalty, or calculated from a short-rate table, to cover the insurer's costs for a policy that ended early. Short-rate refunds commonly apply when the insured cancels, depending on the policy terms.Related:CancellationPro rata cancellationPremium
Signed declination
A client's written confirmation that they were offered a coverage or limit and chose not to buy it, such as sewer backup, earthquake or higher liability limits. Keeping it on file records the client's informed decision and helps manage errors and omissions risk.Related:Errors and omissions (E&O) insuranceRenewalEndorsement
Subjectivity
A condition an insurer attaches to a quote or binder that must be met, usually by a set date, such as a signed application, an inspection report or proof of a sprinkler system. Unmet subjectivities can lead to changed terms or cancellation, so brokers track them closely.Related:SubmissionBinderUnderwriter
Submission
The package of information a broker sends to insurers to request terms on a risk, usually for commercial accounts: the application, schedule of values, loss history, operational details and supporting documents. A clear, complete submission tends to get faster, better responses.Related:UnderwriterSchedule of valuesLoss historySubjectivity

T

Trust accounting
The rules and practices for handling money a brokerage holds for others, mainly client premiums owed to insurers and refunds owed to clients. Provincial requirements generally call for a separate trust account, accurate records and regular reconciliation, and restrict what trust money can be used for. Regulators may review trust records.Related:Agency bill (broker bill)Insurer statement (account current)Reconciliation

U

Umbrella liability
Extra liability coverage that sits above underlying policies, such as auto, home or commercial general liability, and responds once their limits are used up. Some umbrella policies also cover certain claims the underlying policies don't. Available for personal and commercial clients.Related:Commercial general liability (CGL)LimitAccount rounding
Underwriter
The insurer's specialist who evaluates a risk, decides whether to accept it and sets the terms, conditions and premium. Brokers work closely with underwriters on new business, renewals and anything outside their binding authority.Related:SubmissionBinding authorityInsurer

A renewal retention playbook for Canadian brokerages

A practical renewal rhythm: how to sort renewals by risk, set triggers, talk to clients before the insurer does, remarket with a reason and measure retention honestly.

  • Renewals
  • Retention
  • Operations

Retention is decided before the renewal arrives

Most clients don't leave over a single price increase. They leave when the increase arrives without warning, nobody explains it, and someone else calls first. By the time a renewal lands in a CSR's queue a week before expiry, the options are narrow: accept the insurer's terms, scramble to remarket, or hope the client doesn't shop around.

A retention playbook moves that work earlier and makes it predictable. The approach below is written for a typical Canadian brokerage with personal and commercial lines. Adjust the timings to your own book. The principle holds everywhere: decide early which renewals need attention, and give those files to people who have time to act.

Step 1: Sort every renewal before it's urgent

Start with a renewal list run well ahead of expiry, commonly 90 to 120 days for commercial accounts and 45 to 60 days for personal lines. Renewal terms from insurers often arrive closer to expiry, so the first pass is about the client. Price comes later. Sort each renewal into one of three groups:

  • Standard: no material changes, a stable premium history and no open claims. These can follow a light-touch process.
  • Watch: a claim in the past term, a known rate change from the insurer, a change in the client's situation such as a new driver or a renovation, or a long-standing client nobody has spoken to in a year.
  • Remarket: a large expected increase, coverage the client has questioned, a change in the insurer's appetite, or an account large enough to deserve a market review regardless.

The point is to be deliberate. Watch and remarket files go to someone with the time and authority to act. Standard files flow through on schedule.

Step 2: Add triggers to the dates

Dates tell you when to look. Triggers tell you what to do. Agree on a short list of conditions that move a renewal out of the standard process, for example:

  • A premium increase above a percentage or dollar amount you agree internally
  • Any coverage reduction, new exclusion or higher deductible in the renewal terms
  • A claim reported during the term
  • Outstanding underwriting questions or subjectivities on a commercial renewal
  • Missing information, such as an updated building valuation or a renewal questionnaire

Write the triggers down and apply them the same way across producers and CSRs. Consistency is what turns a good habit into a process the brokerage can rely on, including when someone is on holiday.

Step 3: Talk to the client before the insurer does

For watch and remarket files, contact the client early with a short, specific message: the renewal date, what you expect and what you need from them. For example: "Your home policy renews on June 14. Rates in your area are rising, and we'll review your options with you before then. Has anything changed at the property this year?"

A message like this does three jobs. It sets expectations, it collects information you would otherwise chase later, and it shows the client you're working for them. For standard renewals, a well-timed reminder with the renewal date and an easy way to report changes is usually enough. Respect the contact preferences and consent on file, and keep renewal notices factual.

Step 4: Remarket with a reason

Remarketing costs time for your team and for underwriters, so do it when it's warranted. When you do, document why: the trigger, the markets approached, what came back and what you recommended. If the client chooses to stay with their current insurer despite a better option, or declines a coverage you recommended, record the decision and, where your brokerage's practice calls for it, get it in writing. That record protects the client relationship and supports your errors and omissions position.

Step 5: Make the renewal conversation about coverage

A renewal is one of the few moments when clients expect to hear from you. Use it well. Confirm the details on the declarations page, ask about changes to their home, vehicles or business, and look for gaps: a home-based business with no coverage, a newly licensed teenager, no umbrella policy, or a building limit that hasn't kept pace with replacement cost. Clients who see their broker catching these things are far less likely to shop on price alone.

Step 6: Measure retention honestly

Track retention two ways, because each tells a different story:

  • Policy count retention: the share of policies due for renewal in a period that renewed with you.
  • Premium retention: renewed premium as a share of the premium that was up for renewal.

Premium retention that rises while policy retention falls usually means rate increases are masking client losses. Break both measures down by line of business, producer and insurer, and record a lost-business reason every time a client leaves. After a few months, the reasons show where to focus: price, service, coverage or an insurer's appetite.

A simple weekly rhythm

  1. Monday: review the renewal list for the next 120 days and assign new watch and remarket files.
  2. Midweek: work the remarket queue and follow up on outstanding client information.
  3. Friday: check renewals inside 30 days with no confirmed outcome, and escalate anything at risk.
  4. Monthly: review retention by line and producer, and read the lost-business reasons together as a team.

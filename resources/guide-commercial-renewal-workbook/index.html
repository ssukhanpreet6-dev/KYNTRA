The commercial renewal checklist

A checklist and timeline for commercial renewals: a 120-day plan, what to ask the client, schedule of values updates, the market summary and the client meeting.

  • Renewals
  • Commercial lines
  • Retention

Commercial renewals reward preparation. The accounts are larger, the information changes every year and underwriters need time to review it properly. A renewal that starts 30 days out leaves no room to remarket, negotiate or advise. This checklist gives producers and account managers a consistent way to run every commercial renewal, from the first internal review to the signed-off renewal.

Who it's for

Commercial producers, account managers and commercial CSRs at Canadian brokerages, and principals who want every commercial renewal handled the same way, whoever owns the account.

1. Work to a 120-day timeline

  • 120 days out: review the account internally, including last year's terms, claims, open subjectivities and anything the client mentioned during the year
  • 100 days out: send the client your renewal questions and ask for updated values and schedules
  • 75 days out: review the answers and the loss history, and decide whether to remarket
  • 60 days out: send complete submissions to the markets you've chosen
  • 30 days out: compare terms, prepare the market summary and meet the client
  • Before expiry: bind, clear subjectivities and issue documents

Adjust the dates to the size of the account. Larger or harder-to-place risks need more time with underwriters.

2. Ask the client what changed

  • Changes to operations, products or services, including new work outside the current description
  • Revenue and payroll, actual for the past year and estimated for the next
  • Locations added, closed or renovated, with construction and protection details
  • Vehicles and equipment added or sold
  • New contracts, leases or agreements that require specific insurance or certificates
  • Claims and incidents, including anything not yet reported

Send the questions early and in writing, so the client has time to gather answers and you have a record of what they told you.

3. Update the schedule of values

Ask the client to confirm building, contents, equipment and stock values at current replacement cost. Many commercial property policies include a coinsurance clause: if the insured value falls below the required percentage of the actual value, a claim payment can be reduced in proportion. Explain this plainly, and note the values the client confirms.

4. Review loss history before underwriters do

Request loss runs from the current insurer in time to review them. Go through each claim with the client: what happened, what has changed since and what remains open. A submission that explains losses and the steps taken is easier for an underwriter to assess than one that leaves them to find out.

5. Prepare a clean submission

Send each market a complete package: the application, schedules, loss history with explanations and a short account summary. Track every response, and every subjectivity, the conditions to be met before or after binding, through to completion, with an owner for each one.

6. Summarize the market response

Compare quotes on terms as well as premium: limits, deductibles, exclusions, conditions and subjectivities. A side-by-side summary helps the client see what each option covers and why you recommend it.

7. Run the renewal meeting

  • What changed in the business this year
  • The market response and your recommendation
  • Coverage gaps and options, such as higher limits or umbrella liability
  • Payment options and dates
  • Next steps, and who does what

8. Document declined recommendations

When a client declines a recommendation, such as higher limits, an umbrella policy or a coverage extension, record what you recommended, when and why, and ask the client to confirm the decision in writing. It protects the client relationship, and the brokerage, if a claim raises the question later.
